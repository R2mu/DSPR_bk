7.5 Network analysis

Whilst network analysis has been around for a while, it has only recently been more utilized in performance analysis settings. This is namely due to the availability of transactional data, which network analysis is perfectly geared towards. Specifically, network analysis aims to both quantitatively and visually highlight how groups/players interact with each other.

Common measures that we can calculate from network analysis are:

  1. Degree Centrality: This measure indicates how connected a player is within the network. In AFL terms, it represents the number of direct connections (e.g., passes) a player has with others. Example: If Player A passes to or receives passes from 5 different teammates during a match, their degree centrality would be 5.

  2. Betweenness Centrality: This metric identifies players who act as “bridges” between different parts of the network. In AFL, these players might be crucial in linking defense to attack. Example: A midfielder who frequently receives the ball from defenders and then distributes it to forwards would have high betweenness centrality.

  3. Closeness Centrality: This measure represents how quickly a player can interact with all other players in the network. In AFL, it could indicate players who are most efficient at distributing the ball across the entire team. Example: A player with high closeness centrality might be able to effectively pass the ball to any teammate on the field, regardless of their position.

  4. Eigenvector Centrality: This metric takes into account not just how many connections a player has, but also the quality of those connections. In AFL, it could highlight players who connect with other highly influential players. Example: A forward who frequently receives passes from the team’s top midfielders would have high eigenvector centrality.

  5. Clustering Coefficient: This measure indicates how tightly connected a player’s immediate network is. In AFL, it could reveal subgroups or cliques within the team. Example: A high clustering coefficient among defenders might indicate strong coordination and frequent passing between backline players.

  6. Edge Weight: In network analysis, connections (edges) can have different strengths. In AFL, this could represent the frequency or success rate of passes between players. Example: The edge weight between two players who successfully complete 10 passes to each other would be higher than between players who only complete 2 passes.

Visual Representation: Network analysis in AFL can be visually represented through graphs where:

  • Nodes represent players

  • Edges represent passes or other interactions between players

  • The size of nodes might indicate a player’s centrality measure

  • The thickness of edges might represent the frequency or success of interactions

7.7 Simulating data (A superpower)

So as mentioned before having access to transnational data is relatively speaking something we only have had access to more recently. So for a long time whilst, I knew this may be a useful form of analysis as I had no data I didn’t really both fine tuning my skills. However, what I could have done to practice is make a fake data-set via simulation. Whilst, we haven’t discussed data simulation techniques over the previous modules in too much detail, I do strongly encourage you to slowly develop this skill. Luckily, from my experience LLM’s (ChatGPT, Claude etc) are really good at helping with this.

Now one caveat with quick and effective data simulation is you have an idea of what the goal is. For network analysis for example, I know from experience that most of the packages that we will use, require us to have a “From” column and a “To” column. All other columns can be additional information you think is important. However, as you will see later we do need to be a little clever with how we go about analyzing for many teams at once.

7.7.3 Player stats

  • Degree: Total number of connections (both in and out)

  • In-Degree: Number of incoming connections

  • Out-Degree: Number of outgoing connections

  • Betweenness Centrality: Measure of a player’s role in connecting other players

  • Closeness Centrality: Measure of how close a player is to all other players

  • Eigenvector Centrality: Measure of a player’s influence in the network

calculate_player_stats <- function(g, team_name) {
  data.frame(
    Player = V(g)$name,
    Team = team_name,
    Degree =     igraph::degree(g),
    In_Degree =  igraph::degree(g, mode = "in"),
    Out_Degree = igraph::degree(g, mode = "out"),
    Betweenness = round(igraph::betweenness(g,directed = T),2),
    Closeness =   round(igraph::closeness(g),3),
    Eigenvector = round(eigen_centrality(g,directed = T)$vector,2)
  )
}

filter_graph_by_weight <- function(graph, min_weight) {
  # Get edge weights

  keep_edges <- weights >= min_weight

  filtered_graph <- subgraph.edges(graph, which(keep_edges), delete.vertices = FALSE)
  
  return(filtered_graph)

7.10 Association Rules/Market basket analysis

Association rules mining and market basket analysis are data mining techniques used to uncover relationships between items in large data-sets. Originally developed for retail and e-commerce to analyze customer purchasing patterns, these methods can be adapted to work with chains in sport.

The most common algorithm used for this analysis is called Apriori. It works by:

1. Identifying frequent item-sets (groups of items that often appear together)
2. Generating rules from these item sets
3. Evaluating the strength of these rules using metrics like support, confidence, and lift


Metrics

- Support: The frequency of an item-set in the data-set
- Confidence: The likelihood that an item Y is purchased when item X is purchased
- Lift: A measure of how much more likely item Y is purchased when item X is purchased, compared to Y’s usual purchase rate

*These will make sense more in a bit

In a sports context we can use this technique to:

1. Identify patterns in player interactions (e.g., which players tend to pass to each other)
2. Discover play sequences that frequently lead to scoring opportunities
3. Analyze defensive formations that effectively counter certain offensive strategies
4. Uncover unexpected relationships between different aspects of the game

Okay so how do we interpret this.

  1. Rule Interpretation: The rule {Buckley_Kick, Himmelberg_Kick} => {Idun_Kick} means that when Buckley and Himmelberg both kick the ball in a possession chain, it’s often followed by Idun kicking the ball.

  2. Support (0.01): This rule appears in 1% of all possession chains analyzed. It’s a measure of how frequently this combination occurs in the dataset.

  3. Confidence (0.81): When Buckley and Himmelberg both kick in a chain, there’s an 81% chance that Idun will kick next. This high confidence suggests a strong association.

  4. Coverage (0.01): This indicates that the antecedent (Buckley_Kick and Himmelberg_Kick together) occurs in 1% of all chains, regardless of what follows.

  5. Lift (10.66): This is perhaps the most interesting metric. A lift of 10.66 means that Idun is 10.66 times more likely to kick after Buckley and Himmelberg have kicked than he would be in any random situation. This high lift suggests a very strong association that’s unlikely to be random.

  6. Count (17): This specific combination occurred 17 times in your dataset.

In summary, this rule suggests a strong tactical pattern where Buckley and Himmelberg kicking in sequence often leads to Idun getting the next kick. This could indicate a set play or a preferred passing route that the team uses frequently. The high lift and confidence make this a particularly noteworthy pattern that coaches might want to examine further, either to reinforce if it’s successful or to vary if it’s becoming too predictable

You may notice I set the supp to 0.01 and conf to 0.3. This helps limits the amount of rules the algo might find. There is not perfect number to set these, sometimes a bit of trial and error is needed. For example with Sydney I had to set the conf to 0.2.

7.10.1 Adding additional measures.

Lets say you also wanted to incorporate information regarding metres gained per transaction. You could do so with below structure, with the caveat that the way I have formulated metres gained is not ideal.

In simpler terms we are moving from:

  • Standard Apriori: “When Player A passes to Player B, they often pass to Player C next”.

    to

  • Our weclat approach: “The pass sequence from Player A to Player B to Player C is associated with X% of the team’s total metres gained.”

The key difference with our weclat approach is: We’re not just looking at what pass combinations happen frequently, but we’re weighing each occurrence by how many metres were gained. This means we can identify pass sequences that contribute significantly to the team’s overall territory gain, even if they don’t occur as often as other combinations.

For example:

  1. A common but less effective sequence might show up in standard analysis but not be as prominent in our approach.

  2. A less frequent but highly effective sequence might be highlighted by our approach, even if it’s not among the most common patterns.

This method helps us find “high-impact” pass combinations - those that, when they occur, tend to result in substantial gains in field position. It balances frequency with effectiveness, giving us insight into which passing patterns are most valuable for moving the ball down the field.

In AFL terms, this could help identify key play sequences that, while maybe not the team’s go-to moves, are particularly effective when used and might be worth incorporating more often into the team’s strategy.

7.11 Network Analysis vs. Market Basket Analysis in AFL

7.11.1 Similarities

  • Both analyze patterns and relationships in player interactions

  • Can provide insights into team strategies and player roles

  • Use data on passes between players

7.11.2 Differences

7.11.3 Network Analysis

  • Focuses on overall structure of player interactions

  • Considers the entire team as a connected system

  • Identifies key players based on their position in the network

7.11.3.1 Market Basket Analysis

  • Focuses on specific sequences of passes

  • Looks at combinations of events rather than overall structure

  • Identifies frequent and effective pass combinations

7.11.4 Strengths

7.11.4.1 Network Analysis

  • Provides a big-picture view of team dynamics

  • Identifies central players and potential weak links

  • Useful for understanding overall team structure and strategy

7.11.4.2 Market Basket Analysis

  • Uncovers specific, actionable pass sequences

  • Can incorporate effectiveness measures (e.g., metres gained)

  • Good at finding unexpected combinations that lead to success

7.11.5 Weaknesses

7.11.5.1 Network Analysis

  • May overlook specific, effective play sequences

  • Can be complex to interpret for non-technical audiences

  • Doesn’t easily incorporate effectiveness measures

7.11.5.2 Market Basket Analysis

  • Doesn’t provide an overall view of team structure

  • May miss important players who don’t appear in frequent combinations

  • Can generate a large number of rules, making interpretation challenging
